## Executive Summary

This is the book that addresses Amber's exact diagnosis. Her audit of the company found: *"our offers are things people can invest in but there are no connections between them that compels the lead to choose."* That is precisely a money model problem. We have offers. We do not have a **sequence**.

The core idea: a business doesn't grow on one offer, it grows on an ordered set of them — the first one designed to bring cash in fast, the ones after it designed to raise the value of each customer. The measure of a good money model is whether **the cash collected from a new customer in their first 30 days covers what it cost to acquire and serve them.** When it does, growth funds itself and you can spend more than competitors to win the same customer. When it doesn't, every new client is a cash drain and growth is capped by your bank balance.

That last point is the one to internalize: **client-financed acquisition.** It's the difference between "we can afford more ads when we have a good month" and "every sale pays for the next two."

## Structure

Three stages, in order:

1. **Attraction offers** — get cash in the door. Designed for speed of decision and immediate payment, not maximum margin.
2. **Upsell and downsell offers** — raise value at the moment of maximum enthusiasm, and catch the people who said no with something smaller rather than losing them.
3. **Continuity offers** — recurring revenue. Where the actual business lives.

The sequencing matters more than any single offer's quality. A great offer in the wrong position underperforms a mediocre one in the right position.

## The metrics that decide everything

| Metric | What it is | Our status |
|---|---|---|
| **CAC** | Fully loaded cost to acquire one client — ad spend plus sales time | Not measured |
| **COGS / fulfillment cost** | What it actually costs us to deliver a package for a month | **Parked since April 2026** — needs a QuickBooks pull + working session |
| **30-day cash** | What a new client actually pays in their first 30 days | Known per package |
| **LTGP** | Lifetime gross profit per client | Blocked on fulfillment cost |
| **LTGP:CAC** | The ratio that says whether we can scale spend | **Blocked** |

**This is the single biggest hole in the sales system and it has been open since April.** We cannot say whether a $1,500 attraction offer is smart or suicidal without knowing what a client costs to serve. Every offer recommendation until then carries an assumption flag.

## Launchpad's money model — current vs. proposed

### What we have today

Effectively one offer — a monthly retainer at $2,500 / $3,500 / $4,500 — with a free audit floating in front of it and a set of a-la-carte items nobody sequences. **80% of revenue arrives by referral**, which means the money model has never had to work. February 2026 (3 clients lost, 0 signed) is what happens when the referral tap slows and there's no machine behind it.

The July 2026 lower-friction tiers (CRM $197, ads-only from $500, content-only from $750) were the right instinct — widen the entry — but they were added as *more menu items*, not as a sequence. Nothing routes a lead from one to the next.

### Proposed sequence

| Stage | Offer | Cash | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lead magnet** | Growth Audit — free | $0 | Qualify, build trust, create the problem list the next offer solves |
| **Attraction** | The Wrapped Truck / Foundation Day | Cash up front | Get real money in the door fast, get them physically into the campus, cover acquisition cost |
| **Continuity** | The Busy Season Guarantee — $3,500/mo | Recurring | The actual business |
| **Upsell** | Warehouse Week — $9,997 | Large one-time | Anchors the price of everything below it; sold at peak enthusiasm |
| **Downsell** | Content-only $750–$1,500, or CRM $197 | Small recurring | Nobody leaves empty-handed; stays in the ecosystem for later |

Three things this fixes that the current setup doesn't:

1. **The audit finally has a commercial job.** Today it's a free thing we hand out. In a sequence it's the qualifier that earns the waived onboarding fee and sets up the attraction offer.
2. **A no stops being a loss.** The downsell keeps them as a $197 customer instead of a dead lead.
3. **The expensive thing at the top does real work.** Warehouse Week's main function may not be sales volume — it's making $3,500/mo read as reasonable.

### The onboarding fee mechanic

We currently charge no onboarding fee on standard packages, so "waived onboarding" — which appears in the Grand Slam Offer project notes — has nothing to waive.

**Introduce one at $1,000–$1,500 across the board, then waive it for anyone who completes a Growth Audit and signs within 14 days.** This creates a discount without discounting the recurring price, gives the audit a dollar value, and adds urgency. It's the cheapest high-leverage change available and it should be tested first.

### Client-financed acquisition, applied

The question to answer once fulfillment cost is known: **does the attraction offer cover CAC plus first-month delivery?** If a wrapped truck at cost plus a $1,500 foundation fee covers the ad spend that produced the lead plus the first month of service, ad spend becomes self-funding and we can outbid every competitor for the same lead.

If it doesn't, we are buying clients with savings, and the ceiling is the bank account. **This is the arithmetic that decides how fast Launchpad can grow**, and it's currently unanswerable.

## What needs adaptation for an agency

1. **Delivery capacity is the constraint, not inventory.** An info business scales an attraction offer infinitely. Ours consumes a shoot day and a crew. Every attraction offer needs a monthly cap set against Abril's capacity.
2. **Our continuity is the main event, not the back end.** For Hormozi's businesses continuity is often the upsell. For us it *is* the business — the attraction offer exists to feed it.
3. **Cash timing is unusually good for us** — retainers bill monthly in advance. That's an advantage most of his examples don't have. Use it.
4. **Ad spend is pass-through and must stay separate.** Never bundle client ad spend into an offer price; it destroys margin visibility.

---

## Rules for the `offer-builder` skill

1. Never design an offer in isolation — always place it in the sequence (attraction / continuity / upsell / downsell) and name what comes before and after.
2. State the 30-day cash figure for every offer produced.
3. Flag any offer whose 30-day cash doesn't plausibly cover acquisition plus first-month delivery.
4. When fulfillment cost is unknown, say so explicitly rather than assuming — do not produce confident margin claims on missing data.
5. Every attraction offer needs a monthly capacity cap.
6. Every no needs a downsell. A lost deal should exit into a smaller offer, not into nothing.
